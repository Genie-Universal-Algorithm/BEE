Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) models handle long-horizon manipulation, yet success hinges on a few precision-critical phases where millimeter-scale errors undo all prior progress. Online reinforcement learning (RL) can optimize exactly these actions, but free exploration is far too costly on real robots, which makes human corrections indispensable. Existing online RL methods for VLAs either cannot incorporate such corrections or fold them into undifferentiated supervision. Human corrections are not uniformly noisy: they are reliable along some action dimensions and variable along others.

We introduce BEE, an intervention-adaptive framework for real-world RL on a frozen VLA that lets the policy go BEyond Expert imitation. Human corrections are not actions to reproduce, but evidence about a constraint. A Correction Model predicts how a human would correct a given VLA proposal, and how consistent that correction is along each action dimension. This predicted consistency sets the per-dimension tightness of a constraint on policy optimization. Where corrections are consistent the policy stays close to the human; where they vary, the constraint relaxes.

We evaluate BEE on three real-world manipulation tasks and one LIBERO-Pro simulation task at a matched online-data budget. BEE attains the highest success rate on every task, 91.2% on average against 57.5% for RLT and 42.1% for DSRL, and the lowest human intervention rate on all real-world tasks.

Method

BEE keeps the frozen VLA as a proposal and trains only a residual. A Correction Model turns each human takeover into an uncertainty-aware, per-dimension constraint, rather than into an action to copy.

  1. 01 Keep the proposal

    All runs start from open-source π0.5, fine-tuned per task and then frozen. An RLT-style extractor maps its features to a 2048-d latent token. With proprioception, that token is the RL state. The action chunk stays an explicit proposal.

  2. 02 Model the correction

    On a human takeover, the Correction Model predicts the residual relative to the VLA proposal and a variance on each action dimension. The constraint is tight where the correction is consistent, and loose where it varies.

  3. 03 Optimize the residual

    The learned residual is added to the proposal. The update trades off Q-value, staying near the VLA, and a state-dependent multiplier on the dimension-wise constraint.

The residual is not applied to every dimension. Phone charging and cloth aligning are dual-arm joint-space tasks; online RL edits only the seven right-arm joints that perform the precision-critical step, which is also the arm the operator intervenes on. Snack hanging and bowl placing are end-effector tasks; the residual edits pose, and the gripper is held at the VLA proposal. On the two-phase tasks, a progress head trained during supervised fine-tuning hands control from the frozen approach chunk to the online residual.

Tasks

The real-world tasks run on a dual-arm robot with three 480×640 RGB cameras: one on the head and one on each wrist. The frozen VLA sees these views, the language instruction, and proprioception. The online learner sees only the latent token and robot state.

Results

Every method starts from the same fine-tuned π0.5 and is given the same total robot interaction: 90 episodes on each real-world task and 45 on bowl placing. BEE, RLT, SiLRI, and HIL-SERL spend about 20 of those episodes as pre-collected human corrections that seed the demonstration buffer, then 70 online episodes on the real tasks and 25 on bowl placing. DSRL and DAgger have no demonstration buffer, so they spend the same 20 episodes online instead.

During online training the operator takes over only when the robot is about to fail in a way it cannot recover from, so corrections concentrate on the contact phase.

The same budget also rules out methods that do not use the frozen VLA, or that only imitate the operator. On phone charging, DAgger receives 20 extra online episodes in place of the seed corrections and still falls to 63.3 ± 5.8 from the 90% base policy. SiLRI and HIL-SERL train visuomotor policies from scratch; on snack hanging and bowl placing they stay at 0% success and require intervention on more than 90% of steps. A budget of tens of episodes is far below what a from-scratch policy needs on a minute-long task.
